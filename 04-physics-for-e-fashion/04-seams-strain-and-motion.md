# Seams, strain, and motion

**One idea:** Worn circuits fail at two predictable places — where stiff meets soft, and where bodies bend — and both places can be designed around before the first stitch.

> **Previously** — [Electricity meets fabric](01-electricity-meets-fabric.md): the stitched path is part of the circuit. This page is about the path's *mechanical* life. No equations are required today; this is physics of the bend-and-break kind, told straight.

## Metal remembers

Bend a paperclip once: fine. Bend it back and forth twenty times: it snaps — warm at the break, if you touch it quickly. That is **metal fatigue**: each flex rearranges the metal's insides a little, the damage accumulates silently, and one ordinary bend finally finishes what hundreds started.

Conductive thread and fine wires are paperclips in miniature. A garment flexes thousands of times a day — sitting, reaching, dancing — so a stitched circuit is not asked *whether* it can carry flow, but whether it can carry flow *while being gently destroyed at a rate you control*. Well-made e-textiles last years. The control comes from two placement insights.

## Where stiff meets soft

Fabric flexes everywhere; a circuit board, battery holder, or LED module flexes nowhere. So every flex the fabric attempts near a rigid part gets *concentrated* at the exact border where stiff meets soft — the thread there absorbs the garment's whole gesture in a single millimetre. That border is the classic first place e-textiles fail (bench electronics has the same villain: wires break where they exit a plug, never mid-cable).

The remedies are old sewing wisdom holding an engineering title — **strain relief**: anchor rigid parts so they cannot tug their connections (stitch the *module* down firmly with plain thread, letting the conductive stitches carry only electricity, never mechanical load); let paths approach connections with a small slack loop rather than drum-tight; and soften the transition — a dab of flexible glue or an extra fold of fabric at the border spreads the flexing over a wider, forgiving zone.

## Where bodies bend

The second predictable failure zone: **joints**. Elbows, knees, shoulders, waistlines — high-traffic flex territory, hundreds of large bends per hour. Routing is the whole game: paths travel *around* joints (down a side seam rather than across an elbow), or cross them along lines the body folds least. Costume designers solved this centuries before electricity — seams and boning already follow the body's quiet lines — and e-fashion inherits their maps directly. When a path genuinely must cross a joint, it crosses *with slack*: a zigzag or serpentine stitch acts as a tiny spring, straightening and relaxing with each bend instead of stretching taut. (Stretch fabrics make zigzag mandatory — a straight stitch has no give at all, and the first deep stretch snaps it.)

## Mass rides along

One last passenger: weight. A battery swung at the end of a sleeve is accelerated with every gesture — and force is mass times acceleration (`F = m·a`, *the archer's equation in FloralFight*, here moonlighting as a costume-design principle). The same pouch mounted near the body's center barely notices the dance. Heavy parts belong close to the torso, on the garment's strongest seams; light payoffs — LEDs, fibers, sequins — may live way out on the luminous edges. Distribute mass like a choreographer: stillness at the center, sparkle at the extremities.

**Try it (optional, ~2 minutes, nothing to hand-write):** Bend one arm and watch the sleeve: where does the fabric actually fold? Trace, by eye, a path from shoulder to cuff that avoids every fold line. That trace is a professional routing plan — the kind that ships in real wearables.
